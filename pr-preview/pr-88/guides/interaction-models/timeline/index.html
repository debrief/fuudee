    Timeline and narrative¶

    How an analyst reads a plot's narrative along with a replay: narrative entries from every platform, with time-only notes and periods, the comments on fixes and each track's data coverage, in one time-ordered list, the Timeline; how it follows current time without fighting the reader; how entries are chosen, filtered, searched, highlighted, added, changed and deleted; and how narrative is held and imported, from REP files, legacy plots, and Word and PDF documents.

    The product owner decided on 15 Oct 2026 that narrative is read in the Timeline, not a view of its own: one list on one Zulu time axis, which data coverage joined with collation on 16 Oct 2026 (CAP-05, CAP-08; see Collation).

    Requirements cited¶

    SRD Requirement
    CAP-05 An optional temporal view shows narrative entries, time-only annotations and data coverage per source; choosing an entry moves current time to it.
    CAP-19 Narrative is imported from Word and PDF in the legacy parsers' dialects, ported to Python with their tests.
    EXT-04 Narrative documents: Word and PDF, several dialects.
    CON-09, CON-10 The playground runs the same Python in the browser; a capability it lacks is explained, never failed at the point of use.
    CAP-20 Narratives from several platforms are combined on a single Zulu time axis.
    EXT-14 A legacy .dpf plot's narrative is imported, as far as the data model allows.
    NFR-06 Usable with 12,000 narrative entries scaled by three to five: the stress bound. Typical narratives are a few hundred entries (product owner, 15 Oct 2026).
    NFR-13 Everything works from the keyboard, and by touch.
    SUB-19, SUB-21 Every change is a recorded step; undo replays to the step before.
    SUB-04 Times are written by the formatting service, in the analyst's settings.

    Narrative entries¶

    1. An entry is a feature of its own. Kind narrative, with no geometry, as a time-only annotation has: its time (UTC, to the millisecond), its source (who wrote it: a platform, a person or a document, such as BLUE ONE; legacy Debrief's track name on an entry), its entry_type (the source's own kind of entry, free text: legacy logs use GenComment, Posn, CSD, FCS; the sample uses Course, Sonar, CPA, Log), its text (which may run to several lines) and the track it belongs to, track_id, where there is one. Schema 0.0.19.

      One feature an entry, not one feature holding a whole log: each entry added, changed or deleted is then one small step in the history, holding that entry alone. A log of 12,000 entries held as one feature would put all 12,000 into the history at every change. 2. The plot's narrative is every narrative entry in it. There is no narrative layer to create first: entries from a REP file, a legacy plot, the sample's script and the analyst's own typing are one narrative. A legacy plot's narrative layers are kept as named sets of their entries, with the layers' names, as its annotation layers are. 3. One Zulu time axis, one order. By time; at one time by source, ignoring case (and between sources differing only in case, capitals first); from one source at one time, in the plot's order, which is the order its log gave them. Two platforms' entries at one second therefore always come in the same order, whichever log was imported first, and a log's own order at one second is kept. Legacy Debrief sorted entries at one time by source, then type, then text, which put a log's own order at one second out of order, and dropped entries alike in all four; both are kept here. Python and TypeScript are held to the same cases, schemas/vectors/narrative-order.json. 4. Notes and narrative are kept apart. The sample's scripted log, and a legacy plot's narrative entries, were time-only note annotations before schema 0.0.19; they are narrative entries now. A note the analyst adds through the annotation form stays an annotation: the analyst's own remark, with a name, a style and perhaps a period, drawn by the annotation tools, hidden or shown like any annotation. Both are listed in the Timeline. 5. Belonging to a track. An entry imported or added belongs to the plot's track of its source's name, ignoring case and spacing, where there is exactly one; the form and agents may choose another, or none. A track deleted since leaves the link, which is then ignored.

    Importing narrative¶

    1. REP files. ;NARRATIVE: lines (date, time, source, text) and ;NARRATIVE2: lines (date, time, source, type, text) are read as legacy Debrief's ImportNarrative and ImportNarrative2 read them: fields separated by spaces or tabs; the source, and a type, in quotes where they hold spaces; the text the rest of the line, tabs kept, trimmed; dates in the same 1950 to 2049 window as fixes. As in legacy Debrief, a ;NARRATIVE: line has no type: an unquoted source with a space, HMS TORBAY, reads as the source HMS and the text TORBAY…. Unlike legacy Debrief, text run on after a closing quote is kept for the next field rather than dropped with the quote. A line that cannot be read is listed with its number and why. A REP file of narrative alone makes a plot.
    2. Into the plot already open. Read narrative into this plot (REP, Word, PDF)… (on the Timeline's title bar in VS Code; A narrative into the open plot under Import your own in the playground; debrief/import_narrative for agents) reads a second platform's log into the open plot as one step: the file is kept in the plot's item as a source, read-only and hashed, the step's record names the importer, its version and that source (and for a document its dialect and the analyst's choices), and it lists what was not read (fix lines, which belong to an import as a plot of their own, and lines that could not be read, each with why). Undo takes the whole log out again. A Word or PDF document is read in legacy Debrief's dialects, the analyst asked first what legacy Debrief asked: see Word and PDF documents. Import plot file (REP, .dpf, Word, PDF)… makes a plot of a document's narrative alone, as of a REP file's.

    Read in before. A file already read into the plot is known by its content hash, which the step that read it records for its source, whatever the file is called now (product owner, 15 Oct 2026); an entry already in the plot by its time (to the millisecond), source and text, compared as the importer matches a platform to a track: ignoring case and spacing, the source as written or as the name of the track it belongs to. Before anything else is asked, the analyst is told, and offered Skip entries already in the plot or Cancel:

    • in VS Code, a Quick Pick titled copy.docx is already in the plot, its line saying when and by whom it was read in, as which step, and how many of its entries are in the plot (Read in on 15 Oct 2026 09:30:00Z by Ian (step 4, Narrative read from red-one-log.docx). 5 of its 7 entries are in the plot.); the skip says how many entries are not, or Nothing new to read;
    • in the playground, the same line in red at the top of the reading form, whose action becomes Skip entries already in the plot; Cancel reads nothing;
    • for agents, debrief/preview_narrative says it (earlier: each step that read the file, with at, by, how and the source's name; already: how many of its entries are in the plot), and debrief/import_narrative refuses such a file, nothing read, with code -32003 and a message saying the same, until duplicates is "skip" (read only the entries not in the plot) or "add" (read them all again; the record says the plot has them twice).

    The same applies when the file is new but some of its entries are already in the plot, from another file or by hand (3 of the 7 entries in blue-log.docx are already in the plot). Skipped entries are listed in the step's record as not read again; the source already kept is the step's source, not kept twice. A read since undone is not in the plot, so it is not said; a file of the same name with other content is still refused, to be renamed. 8. Legacy plots (.dpf). Narrative entries come in with their time, source, type and text; the entry type, which the Phase 2 import listed as not imported, is kept.

    The Timeline¶

    1. One component, every host. VS Code shows the Timeline as a view in the Debrief sidebar; the playground shows the same component in its Timeline panel, which folds away like every side panel. It replaced a native tree, which could not search, filter, follow current time without taking the keyboard, or hold sixty thousand entries.

    2. A quiet table. Column headings once, Time, Source, Type, Entry; then a row an entry, with nothing labelled in the rows:

      Time Source Type Entry
      15 Jun 2023
      08:29:00Z ● BLUE ONE Sonar Bow sonar contact, bearing 277.0°T. Classifying.
      08:30:00Z ● BLUE ONE Course Came to 215.0°T, 10.0 kn.
      08:58:00Z EXCON Log Permission granted.
      • The time in the editor's monospaced font, written by the formatting service in the analyst's settings; a day's date heads its first entry.
      • The source, with its track's colour where it belongs to one; the type quieter; the text wrapping, with its line breaks.
      • A note shows its name as its source and Note as its type, a period Period and Until … before its text, a fix comment its track and Fix. One hidden on the map says so.
      • Data coverage (CAP-05, CAP-08; decision, product owner, 16 Oct 2026): each period a track has data for, from its first fix to its last and broken at gaps as collation breaks them, cut where the catalogue item supplying it changes. Its source is that item (the plot's own title for the data it was imported with), its type Data, its text the track and how many fixes, Until … before them: Until 12:00:00Z. BLUE ONE: 241 fixes. In the track's colour; hidden on the map, it says so. Choosing it moves current time to its start. Worked out in TypeScript from the plot alone, as Python works out coverage, held to the same vectors (schemas/vectors/collation.json).
      • Entries outside the time window are drawn fainter: still read, still chosen.
      • Where the list is narrow (VS Code's sidebar, the playground's side column, a phone), time, source and type are one line and the text goes under them, aligned with the source, so the time is a gutter down the left. The page never scrolls sideways.
      • Following current time. The entry current time is at (the last at or before it, among those listed) is marked by a bar in the theme's highlight and a quiet ground. As current time moves, while playing or stepping or from a tool, the list keeps that entry in sight: it scrolls only when the entry leaves the middle of the view (its top tenth to three quarters down), and then puts it a third of the way down, so what comes next is seen below it. It never moves the keyboard, and never animates, so a reader is not pulled about.

      The reader always wins. Scrolling the list (wheel, touch, scrollbar) or moving through it with the keyboard stops it following. Follow time then appears in the bar, with an arrow towards where current time is (↑ or ↓); it starts following again and brings the entry into sight. Choosing an entry also starts following again, since current time moves to it, and so does pressing Play (product owner, 15 Oct 2026): playing is asking to read along. Scrolling away while it plays stops it following again, as at any other time; pausing leaves it as it is.

      Following starts afresh, at current time, when another plot is opened; the same plot shown again after a change (an entry added, an undo) keeps the reader's place. The host says which plot it is (plot_id on show_timeline). 12. Choosing an entry (click, tap, Enter or Space) moves current time to it, or to the start of a period. Outside the window shown, the window widens or moves to take it in, shown but never saved, exactly as for a tool's time, with Keep and Revert on the time controller (CAP-28). Where several entries share a second, the one chosen is the one marked, not the last of them. 13. Filter and search. Filter opens checkboxes, with counts, for each kind (Narrative, Notes and periods, Fix comments, Data coverage), source and type; unticked ones are left out, and Filter (2) says how many are. Show all puts them back. The search box finds text in an entry's text, source or type, ignoring case, and marks it; Escape clears it. Under the list, 12 entries of 120 while anything is left out. The filter and the search are the reader's own (product owner, 15 Oct 2026), kept in the page (VS Code's webview state; the browser's storage in the playground, every read and write guarded), never in the plot: two analysts reading one narrative want different filters, and neither changes what the plot says. Following and current time work among the entries listed. Agents filter for themselves (debrief/narrative); debrief.timelineEntries answers for the whole plot, so its current is the entry current time is at among all of them, which is the row the reader sees marked unless their filter leaves it out. 14. Adding, changing and deleting an entry. + (or N with the list focused; Add narrative entry… in VS Code) opens a short form above the list: Time (starting at current time, in any of the analyst's notations: 09:30Z, 15 Jun 2023 09:30:00Z, 150930Z JUN 23), Source and Type (each suggesting those the plot has), Track (following the source's name until chosen) and Entry. Edit and Delete appear on the row being worked with (the one chosen, or the keyboard's), so the list stays quiet; F2 or double-click changes, Delete deletes. Each is one step, named in the History view (Narrative entry by BLUE ONE at 08:40:00Z added, …: text changed, … moved to 08:41:00Z, … deleted), undone like any other: Undo in the History view, or Ctrl+Z (Cmd+Z on macOS) from the Timeline itself, as from the map, the Outline or the History view, except in the search box and the form's text boxes, where it undoes the typing (product owner, 15 Oct 2026). Ctrl+Shift+Z or Ctrl+Y redoes. Deleting from the keyboard keeps its place, on the entry after the one deleted. A form that cannot be saved says why and stays open; a change made elsewhere meanwhile is never overwritten (SUB-28). 15. On the map. The entry current time is at, where it belongs to a track, is shown beside that track's square: its source in bold and the start of its text, in a small box in the theme's colours, never in the way of the pointer, to the left where the right is short of room. It goes when ten minutes of plot time pass with no later entry for a track, so an entry long past does not linger. The map is never sent the narrative itself: a plot may hold sixty thousand entries.

      Narrative on the map, a box under the list, turns it off and on (product owner, 15 Oct 2026). It is the plot's choice, not the reader's: kept in the plot's island (PlotView.narrative_on_map, left out meaning on), like the Tote's Lines on the map, so it travels with the plot file; never a step, never undone (SUB-22). A colleague's change meanwhile is never overwritten (SUB-28). 16. The outline has one row, Narrative: 120 entries from 3 sources, which shows the Timeline; the entries are not listed there one by one (product owner, 15 Oct 2026: kept as it is).

    What is a step, and what isn't¶

    Step in the history Undone by Ctrl+Z
    Adding, changing or deleting an entry Yes, one each Yes
    Reading a REP file's narrative into the plot Yes, one for the file Yes
    Choosing an entry (current time) No No
    Filtering, searching, following No (the reader's own, in the page) No
    Highlights, text size, wrapping, columns, the choosing switch No (the reader's own, in the page) No
    Reading a Word or PDF narrative into the plot Yes, one for the document Yes
    Narrative on the map, off or on No (the plot's island) No

    Keyboard and touch¶

    In the Timeline Keys Touch
    Move through the list (stops following) Up, Down, Page Up, Page Down, Home, End swipe
    Move current time to the entry Enter or Space tap
    Change the entry F2 or E tap it, then Edit
    Delete the entry Delete tap it, then Delete
    Add an entry N or Insert +
    Search /; Escape clears; Down back to the list the box
    The reader's view (highlights, choosing, text, columns) Tab to ⋯, Enter; Escape closes ⋯
    Close the form Escape; Ctrl+Enter saves Cancel
    Undo or redo the plot's last step Ctrl+Z; Ctrl+Shift+Z or Ctrl+Y (Cmd+Z, Cmd+Shift+Z on macOS), anywhere in the Timeline but a text box Undo in History

    The list is a listbox for screen readers: each entry read with its full date and time, its source, type and text; the keyboard's place is its active option, the entry current time is at its current one (aria-current="time").

    Ctrl+Z is decided in the Timeline's page as the key is pressed, against what has focus then: in a text box it is left to the text; elsewhere the page asks its host to undo (undo_asked, redo_asked in VS Code, which runs Undo as the map's Ctrl+Z does, and only when there is something to undo). A context key that a keybinding read would reach the page's host after focus had moved, so a Ctrl+Z straight after clicking into the search box could undo the plot.

    The reader's view: highlights, choosing, text and columns¶

    1. One small menu, so the bar stays quiet. Legacy Debrief's Narrative viewer had extras the product owner kept (15 Oct 2026): phrases highlighted, an off switch for choosing an entry moves current time, the text's size and a wrap toggle, and which columns show. They are in a menu, ⋯ in the bar beside Filter, a box under the bar like the filter's, never a row of controls above the list:

      In the menu What it does
      Highlight words, phrases, separated by commas Each word or phrase is marked wherever it is in an entry's text, ignoring case: bold, underlined in the theme's highlight, so it is told from what the search found (shaded) without colour alone. The longest phrase at a place wins (contact lost whole, not contact); the search wins where the two meet. Twenty phrases at most. A row with one is read as …, highlighted by a screen reader.
      Choosing an entry moves current time On, as before. Off, choosing an entry (click, tap, Enter) puts the keyboard's place on it, showing its Edit and Delete, and leaves current time, and the list's following, as they were: for reading back through a log while a replay holds its place.
      Wrap long entries On, as before. Off, each entry is one line, cut with an ellipsis; the whole text is still read by a screen reader and shown in the form.
      Text 12px, A−, A+ The list's text from 11 to 18 px; the bar and the menu keep theirs.
      Columns: Time, Source, Type The columns shown; the entry's text always is. Narrow, with the time hidden, the text starts at the left edge.

      ⋯ is drawn like Filter (2) when anything differs from the Timeline's own view. Escape closes the menu, back to its button. Like the filter, the view is the reader's own (product owner, 15 Oct 2026): kept in the page (VS Code's webview state; the browser's storage in the playground, guarded), never in the plot, never sent to the host. The page never scrolls sideways at any of these settings, at 210, 280 or 390 px, in a wider font than this machine's (the tests' check).

    Scale (NFR-06)¶

    Typical narratives are a few hundred entries; the product owner rarely sees more (15 Oct 2026). That case must be quick, and is:

    Where What, at 2,000 entries Time
    Python, desktop One entry changed; undone about 20 ms; 10 to 20 ms
    Playground (Pyodide) A 2,000-line log read into an open plot and shown 0.8 s
    Playground An entry added through the form, saved, the plot opened again and the list shown; undone the same way 190 to 250 ms; 220 to 250 ms

    The playground's round trip is mostly the plot opened again after the step (read from Python, the map and every panel redrawn), as for any step: 115 to 160 ms with 36 entries.

    The stress bound stays the legacy sample's 12,000 entries scaled by three to five, measured with 60,000 synthetic entries:

    Where What Time
    Python, desktop One step adding all 60,000; the plot file 20 MB, 35 MB with its history 1.4 to 2.5 s
    Python, desktop One entry changed; undone (12,000 entries: 0.11 s; 0.08 s) 0.7 to 1.0 s; 0.5 to 0.6 s
    Python, desktop Ordered; a query by source and text (debrief/narrative) 0.2 to 0.6 s; 0.3 s
    Timeline logic Ordered; filtered, rowed and laid out; searched 50 ms; 40 ms; 13 ms
    Playground (Pyodide) A 60,000-line log read into an open plot and shown 8 to 30 s
    Playground The list built; rows drawn 20 to 90 ms (0.4 s on a busy machine); 15 to 20
    Playground Scrolling by the wheel: time between frames p50 and p95 16.7 ms (60 a second)
    Playground A search through all of them 30 to 60 ms

    A change rewrites the whole plot file, history included, and checks it whole before it is written: that is the design (one file, history included, 13 Oct 2026), and at 60,000 entries it costs most of a second. The review of 15 Oct halved it with small changes (the plot's period read from its earliest and latest times only; a plain narrative entry checked by hand before its JSON is hashed; the file read and hashed once a change, not twice); anything more, such as checking or hashing only what changed, is not worth building for narratives of a few hundred entries.

    Only the rows in sight are drawn (with 400 px above and below), each measured once drawn; the rest are estimated from their text's length and the column's width, and the row at the top of the view stays put when estimates become measurements. Two things made the Python side fast enough: JSON Schema checks each feature against its own kind's class (narrative entries by an exact hand-written check that is never more lenient than the schema, a test holds it to that), instead of trying every feature class in turn (37 s for 20,000 entries before, 1 s after); and a history keeps the plot it built while the file is unchanged.

    Word and PDF documents (CAP-19)¶

    Legacy Debrief read narrative from Word (.doc, .docx) and PDF in four dialects, in Debrief.ReaderWriter.Word. They are ported to Python in debrief_io.word, with the cases of their tests on synthetic documents (DEV-05: none of legacy Debrief's documents or their text is copied), and checked against legacy Debrief's own expectations on its sample documents where a checkout of it is at hand (DEBRIEF_LEGACY): the same 13, 5, 18, 364, 350, 371 and 285 entries, the same 15 Rider entries and 28 ASW positions.

    Dialect Known by Read as
    Narrative log (ImportNarrativeDocument, and ImportWord_LEGACY before it) anything not below; .doc, .docx and PDF An entry a paragraph, DTG,day,month,year,platform,type,text, the DTG HHMM or DDHHMM, the metadata usually hidden text. A short entry (DDHHMM text, HHMM text) takes the date and platform from the entry before, its type FCS if it starts with a track number, otherwise none (legacy N/A; the Timeline's filter lists entries with none as (no type)). Text alone continues the entry before, every line of it up to the next entry (product owner, 15 Oct 2026), so a PDF's long entry, a line a paragraph, is whole; past 2,000 lines, a runaway document, the rest is listed as not read. 12 Mar 20 marks a new day. START OF RECORDS and END OF RECORDS FOR bound the entries in a longer document. A line starting with a number is a short entry only if its time is in order: see below.
    Rider's narrative (ImportRiderNarrativeDocument) a first table whose first cell is DTG Start Its second row the start DTG (220000Z JUL 09, to the hour) and, fifth, the platform; from its fourth row an entry a row: time of day, bearing (273, 92/112, an arc 096-112, No B), beam, text. Every entry of type Rider, written [Brg:92/112, Beam:12/13] text as legacy Debrief wrote it.
    ASW data (ImportASWDataDocument) five TMPOS lines in its first hundred Positions of one platform: a track, not narrative. Imported as a plot of its own, the track named as the analyst says (legacy Debrief asked); refused, saying so, when read into an open plot. TIMPD gives the year.
    Dated narrative (ImportWordDummy) a line yyyy,MM,dd,HH:mm[:ss], Type, platform and text after the date. Legacy Debrief never offered it; read here rather than misread as a narrative log.

    The corrections legacy Debrief made are made, and said in the step's record: a day that goes backwards with no new month or year (a mangled cut and paste) keeps the day before; an entry at 23xx whose hidden day is one after its DTG's (logged just after midnight about the evening before) takes the DTG's day; a six-figure short entry whose day is earlier than the last is in the next month. Two more, for a log with no metadata (a PDF), new here: a four-figure short entry more than twelve hours before the entry before it is past midnight, on the next day (legacy Debrief put it on the same day, almost a day back), said in the record; and a short entry at 23xx just after an entry past midnight (00xx) is a late entry on the evening before, as legacy Debrief corrected a full entry's day (it put a six-figure one a month on).

    A number at the start of a line is not always a time (product owner, 15 Oct 2026). Legacy Debrief read any line starting with four or six figures as a short entry, so 2000 yds, closing. was an entry at 20:00. Here such a line, with no metadata, is an entry only when its time, after the day corrections above, is in order with the entries around it; otherwise it is text of the entry before, and the step's record lists it (1 line starting with a number was read as text of the entry before, not as an entry: its time would be out of order with the entries around it (line 12: "2000 yds, closing.")). Out of order is either:

    • behind: more than an hour before the entry before it (or a day marker after that entry). A late entry is written up within the hour; one dated by the late-entry rule is in order. A day rolling over is in order: a six-figure DTG gives the day, a day marker gives it, and a four-figure time more than twelve hours back is past midnight.
    • ahead: more than an hour after the entry after it, that being the earliest of the next three entries that are not themselves more than an hour before the entry before. The earliest, so that numbers in a row (2000 yds, 2100 yds) do not vouch for each other; a late entry after it, within the hour, does not count against it.

    An entry with its metadata (DTG,day,month,year,platform,type,text) is never text, however out of order: its metadata says it is an entry. So that one entry out of order does not take the rest of the log with it, a line more than an hour behind is still an entry when the next entries, two at least, are all with it and all more than an hour before the entry before: it was the entry before that was out of order. The limits, said plainly: the last line of a log has nothing after it, so a number there is checked against the entry before only; a run of four or more numbers, each well ahead, vouch for each other and are entries, as legacy Debrief read them, and the log goes on from the entry after them; a genuine late entry written more than an hour late, followed by entries in order, is text. Legacy Debrief's sample logs have none of these: their counts are unchanged.

    FCS entries are read as legacy Debrief read them (bearing, range in kiloyards, yards or metres, course, speed, classification, track number, the solution's source) and kept as narrative: TMA solution positions on the platform's track wait for TMA solutions in the data model (Phase 4, D stream), and the record says how many there were. A Rider entry's bearing, with its ambiguous twin, is also a cut of the sensor Rider bearings on the platform's track, as legacy Debrief made it (product owner, 24 Oct 2026; sensors and bearings); the entry keeps it in its text.

    Where this differs from legacy Debrief, deliberately: times are UTC throughout (legacy Debrief read some in the machine's time zone); a day or time that does not exist is listed, not rolled on (legacy's 31 April was 1 May); a long type missing its comma keeps its whole first word (legacy cut its last letter); every line continuing an entry is joined to it (legacy joined six, dropped the seventh and went on joining; product owner, 15 Oct 2026); a bad line or Rider row is listed with its number and why, and reading goes on (legacy stopped some imports at the first); a Rider log that passes midnight goes on into the next day (legacy kept the first day); an entry's type N/A is no type. A day marker's two-figure year follows the entries' rule (after 80, 19xx).

    What the analyst is asked, as legacy Debrief asked, before a document is read into the open plot (debrief/preview_narrative says what the file holds, changing nothing):

    • which types of entry to read, with how many of each, all ticked, when there are several;
    • all entries, or only those within the plot's period (its tracks' first to last fix), when some fall outside;
    • which of the plot's tracks a platform is, for each of up to five platforms with no track of their name (legacy Debrief's prefixes HMS, Hms, USS, RNAS and HNLMS are skipped before matching: HMS BLUE ONE is BLUE ONE's).

    An entry whose platform is matched to a track, by its name or by the analyst's answer, has the track's name as its source, and so its colour (product owner, 15 Oct 2026): HMS BLUE ONE's entries are BLUE ONE's. The name as the document wrote it is kept in the step's record (9 entries written as from HMS BLUE ONE are BLUE ONE's). A platform matched to no track keeps its name as written.

    The answers are remembered, for each dialect, as the analyst's own setting (product owner, 15 Oct 2026): the types not read, whether only within the period, and the track (by name) each platform was said to be. In VS Code they are kept in the user's configuration file (debrief-config's narrative_import, configuration version 3), in the playground in the page's settings; never in a plot or a catalogue, and the importer is only given the answers. Next time a document of that dialect is read, they are offered: in VS Code a first Quick Pick, Read as last time with the answers in a line, chosen by Enter, or Choose again; in the playground the form comes filled in, the answers said above it, with Read taking the keyboard. A type not seen before is read; a platform not remembered, or whose track the plot has not, is asked about.

    VS Code asks in Quick Picks, one after another; the playground in one form. An agent passes the answers to debrief/import_narrative (types, within_period, tracks, platform, and duplicates for a file in the plot already) or reads everything. A PDF of a log names no platform (its metadata was hidden text, not printed): its entries' source is the plot's one track, as legacy Debrief took it, or else the document's name, or platform where an agent gives it.

    Reading the files. Documents come from elsewhere, so each reader has hard limits and refuses, saying which, what passes them rather than exhaust the host's memory or time: a file over 200 MB (before it is read into memory); a .docx whose zip directory is over 4 MB, whose text part unpacks to over 64 MB, or whose XML nests over 1,000 deep; a .doc claiming more text than its stream holds, or whose piece table is out of order; a PDF of over 10,000 pages, a page drawing over 4 MB of instructions or showing text over 20,000 times, a stream unpacking to over 32 MB, over 5 million operations in all, or taking over 60 seconds; and, for all, over 500,000 paragraphs or 20 million characters, far more than any narrative. .docx with the standard library (zip and expat, refusing document type declarations, as the .dpf reader does); .doc with olefile (pure Python, for the compound file), its text from the piece table and its tables' rows from the paragraph properties, read in debrief_io.documents; PDF with pypdf (pure Python), a line of text a line. A file is known by its first bytes, not its name: legacy Debrief met .docx files named .doc. All three run in Pyodide, so the playground reads Word and PDF too, in the browser; the Pyodide spike holds the browser's reading to the desktop's. A Word 6 or 95 document, a password-protected one, a damaged one, and a PDF with no text (a scan, which needs text recognition first) are refused, saying which. python-docx is used only to build the tests' synthetic documents.
